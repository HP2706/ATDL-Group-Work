\begin{abstract}
We replicate a subset of the experiments in ``Deep Double Descent: Where Bigger Models and More Data Hurt'' by Nakkiran et al. Using the authors' released ResNet18 and five-layer CNN definitions inside our own PyTorch training pipeline, we reproduce width sweeps, training trajectories and sample-size comparisons on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, and compare them with the authors' released measurements. We further re-implement the IWSLT'14 Transformer experiments. To fit our budget, we train for at most a tenth of the original horizon with one seed per setting (229 vision runs, 14.98 B200 GPU-hours). Model-wise double descent under label noise, and the shift of the interpolation threshold with noise, augmentation and sample size, are reproduced qualitatively. Epoch-wise double descent is replicated for sufficiently large models, while small and medium sized models follow a monotone and U-shaped test error trajectories. The CNN plateau, where more data does not help, does not appear at the authors' widths, which we attribute to the fixed update budget. The noise-free peaks reported for CIFAR-100 are not confirmed at our horizons. [Translation: one sentence missing??] 
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Classical learning theory predicts a U-shaped bias-variance trade-off, where larger models overfit beyond some complexity. Modern networks contradict this, generalising better as they grow far beyond the number of training samples. Which regime applies matters in practice. It affects whether to enlarge a model, train it longer, stop early or collect more data.
\\ \\
Nakkiran et al.~\cite{nakkiran2021deepdouble} reconcile the two views through the \emph{effective model complexity} (EMC) of a training procedure: the largest number of samples it can fit to near-zero training error (Section~\ref{sec:background}). They hypothesize that test error behaves atypically when the EMC is close to the number of training samples $n$. Varying the width, the training time or $n$ moves a model through this critical regime. The results are \emph{model-wise} and \emph{epoch-wise} double descent, and \emph{sample-wise} non-monotonicity, where more data
can hurt. The authors demonstrate these effects for CNNs, ResNets and Transformers on CIFAR-10/100 and machine translation. We test five of their claims:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[C1] Model-wise double descent occurs with the test-error peak at the interpolation threshold.
  \item[C2] Label noise, data augmentation and more training samples move this threshold, and the peak, to larger models.
  \item[C3] Small, intermediate and large models show respectively monotone, U-shaped and double-descent test-error trajectories over training time.
  \item[C4] Near the threshold, more training data does not help and can increase test error.
  \item[C5] Optimal early stopping largely removes these peaks
\end{enumerate}

Our contributions are threefold. First, we wrote an independent PyTorch pipeline for training, evaluation and checkpointing around the authors' released model definitions. Second, we replicate the model-, epoch- and sample-wise vision experiments on CIFAR-10/100 and compare them directly with the authors' released measurements. We also re-implement the IWSLT'14 Transformer experiments of their Figures~3 and~11(b). Third, we report the compute cost of this reduced protocol. In summary, C1 and C2 are reproduced qualitatively, C3 and C4 only partially, and our results are
consistent with C5.

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}
A training procedure $\mathcal{T}$ maps a training set
$S=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ to a classifier $\mathcal{T}(S)$. It covers
architecture, optimizer, learning-rate schedule, number of steps and
augmentation. For a data distribution $\mathcal{D}$ and tolerance
$\epsilon>0$, the EMC is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{EMC}_{\mathcal{D},\epsilon}(\mathcal{T})
  = \max\bigl\{n \mid \mathbb{E}_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^n}
    [\mathrm{Error}_S(\mathcal{T}(S))] \le \epsilon\bigr\},
  \label{eq:emc}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{Error}_S$ is the error on the training set itself and the
authors use $\epsilon=0.1$. Their generalized double-descent hypothesis distinguishes three regimes. If $\mathrm{EMC}\ll n$ (under-parameterized) or $\mathrm{EMC}\gg n$ (over-parameterized), any change that increases the EMC decreases test error. If $\mathrm{EMC}\approx n$ (critically parameterized), such a change may either increase or decrease it. The \emph{interpolation threshold} is the point where $\mathrm{EMC}=n$. Since the EMC cannot be computed directly, we follow the authors and estimate the threshold as the smallest width $\hat{k}_{\mathrm{interp}}$ whose final training error on the (possibly noisy) training labels is at most $\epsilon$.

Increasing width or training time raises the EMC and moves a model through the threshold. Increasing $n$, adding label noise or adding augmentation makes the training set harder to fit and shifts the threshold to larger models. Model-, epoch- and sample-wise double descent (C1, C3, C4) are thus one-dimensional slices through the same picture, and C2 describes how that picture shifts.

\section{Methodology and experimental design}

\subsection{Vision setup}
\subsubsection{Reduced Training}

To cover several experimental settings within our compute budget, we
reduced Adam training from 4,000 to 400 epochs and CIFAR-10 SGD training
from 500,000 to 50,000 updates. These are 10\% of the original experiment;
the CIFAR-100 CNN run instead uses 5\% of the stated one-million SGD steps. 

\subsubsection{Model implementation}
The original study uses three architecture families: width-scaled
ResNet18, a five-layer CNN, and a Fairseq Transformer. Our completed
experiments train the two vision families from random initialization.
We reused the authors' model definitions of
\href{https://gitlab.com/harvard-machine-learning/double-descent/-/blob/0d653296f870e6bf7ee82dc71b4f7b49d53fa85d/models/resnet18k.py}{ResNet18}
and the
\href{https://gitlab.com/harvard-machine-learning/double-descent/-/blob/0d653296f870e6bf7ee82dc71b4f7b49d53fa85d/models/mcnn.py}{five-layer CNN},
rather than reimplemting the architectures. Both scale convolutional
channels as $[k,2k,4k,8k]$. ResNet18 uses two preactivation residual
blocks per stage; the CNN uses four convolutional stages with batch normalization and ReLU, spatial max pooling, and a final linear classifier. For the NLP experiment we use the same Transformer implementation as the paper, namely the fairseq Transformer
\href{https://github.com/facebookresearch/fairseq/tree/df2f84ce619edddb80e720a56abc74d5490fed99}{Fairseq v0.9.0}.
Its six-layer encoder and decoder use eight attention heads, feed-forward
width $4d_{model}$, sinusoidal positions, and post-normalization.

We wrote our own PyTorch training code, data loading, evaluation,
resumable checkpointing, and concurrency primitives that allow us to
manage multiple training runs on the same GPU.


Our loader scales pixels
to $[0,1]$ without channel normalization, samples one fixed corruption
mask per seed, and replaces selected labels with uniformly sampled
incorrect classes. This noise procedure follows Section~4 of the
paper, which specifies that label noise is sampled once rather than
at each epoch~\cite{nakkiran2021deepdouble}. Subsets use a seeded
permutation, implementing the paper's random subsampling reproducibly.
Augmentation, when enabled, uses four-pixel padding, a random
$32\times32$ crop, and horizontal flipping, as specified in
Appendix~B.2. All augmentations of an image retain the same training
label.

Scaling pixels by $1/255$ is our preprocessing default; the paper and
released model and plotting code do not establish whether the authors
used channel normalization. This is a simple, reproducible choice that
bounds the input values and keeps the same transformation across
datasets, subset sizes, and noise settings without estimating
additional channel statistics. We cannot verify that it matches
the authors' preprocessing, and normalization could affect optimization
and the resulting curves.

We evaluate the training subset without augmentation against its fixed
training labels and the full 10,000-image test split against clean
labels. Mean cross-entropy and misclassification fractions are recorded
every ten epochs for Adam and every 1,250 updates for SGD, for comparison
with the authors' unchanged numeric results.


\subsubsection{Training and optimizers}
All reported runs use batch size 128 as the paper, cross-entropy loss, seed
zero, FP32 training, and no weight decay.

Adam runs use a constant
learning rate $10^{-4}$ with PyTorch's default moments
$(\beta_1,\beta_2)=(0.9,0.999)$ and $\epsilon=10^{-8}$.
SGD uses no momentum and our explicitly implemented schedule
\[
  \eta_t=\frac{0.1}{\sqrt{1+\lfloor t/512\rfloor}},
\]
where $t$ is the number of completed updates.

This implements the
paper's inverse-square-root learning rate schedule. Adam metrics and checkpoints are saved every ten epochs, and SGD
metrics and checkpoints every 1,250 updates, yielding 40 measurements
per completed run. Checkpoints preserve model, optimizer, random-number
state, and the data position for resumption after interruption.

\subsubsection{Hidden Dimension size}

The original paper does a sweep of each dimension from 1-128. In order to save compute we sweep a much smaller grid of $\{2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, 64\}$ for Resnet18 and $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, 64\}$ for CNN.
For the full-data CNN trained with augmentation we use
$\{1,\allowbreak 2,\allowbreak 3,\allowbreak 4,\allowbreak 6,\allowbreak
8,\allowbreak 12,\allowbreak 16,\allowbreak 24,\allowbreak 32,\allowbreak
36,\allowbreak 40,\allowbreak 48,\allowbreak 64,\allowbreak 96,\allowbreak
128\}$.

The reason behind the non uniform  grid-selection comes down to certain figures relying on a specific width, that thus has to be added to the grid.

We include a full comparison of our setup with the paper's stated
protocol and released code in Appendix~\ref{app:protocol},

\subsection{Translation setup}
\label{sec:translation-setup}

\subsubsection{Experiment selection}
For the translation experiments we choose to try to reproduce Figure 3 and Figure 11b in the paper. They both use the IWSLT'14 German $\rightarrow$ English dataset and the same transformer family. They contain complimentary experiments: Figure 3 varies $d_{model}$ while comparing 4k and 18k samples, and Figure 11b holds $d_{model}$ fixed at 64 and 80 and varies the sample size. Together they test the claim of "more data can hurt" from two directions, and they also test whether the phenomenons of model-wise double descent and sample-wise non-monotonicity in a different domain. 

\subsubsection{Experimental setup and reproduction choices}
The description of the experimental setup is limited, but the authors describe that they use the IWSLT'14 German $\rightarrow$ English dataset. They train a 6-layer encoder-decoder Transformer with 8 attention heads. The model size varies in the experiments with $d_{model}$, and the feed-forward dimension is $d_{ff}=4d_{model}$. They use 10\% label smoothing and no dropout, and they train for 80k gradient updates. The IWSLT text was preprocessed using Byte-Pair-Encodings, and they used the Fairseq sequence-modeling toolkit for the Transformer implementation. Training follows the optimization procedure of Vaswani et al.~\cite{vaswani2017attention}, with a linear warmup phase followed by inverse-square-root learning-rate decay using Adam. \\

Because the experiments were not fully specified in the report nor in their code repository, so we had to make some choices. We used the optimization procedure of Vaswani et al. \cite{nakkiran2021deepdouble}, using Adam with $(\beta_1,\beta_2)=(0.9,0.98)$ and $\epsilon=10^{-9}$. We used 4000 warmup steps, and learning rate $lr=d_{model}^{-0.5}\cdot\min(step\_num^{-0.5},step\_num\cdot warmup\_steps^{-1.5})$. So the learning rate increases gradually during the warmup, and then decays proportional to the square root of the step number. \\
For batches we used a max token budget of $4096$, rather than a fixed number of sentences. Batches are then formed from similarly sized sentences using a Fairseq-like length-based batching procedure. Therefore the number of sentences per batch vary with sentence length. \\

For figure 3 we used 25 widths, evenly spaced from 8 to 200, so $d_{model}=\{8,16,...,192,200\}$, with 4k and 18k training samples. For figure 11b we used $d_{model}=\{64,80\}$ and $n=2k,4k...,48k,50k$. So our setup is Fairseq-like, but we do not know for sure if it is exactly the same as the authors.

%% Epoch-wise double descent (C3)
\subsection{Epoch-wise Double Descent}
\label{sec:epochwise}
Nakkiran et al. argue that traning time acts like model size. As traning proceeds the EMC grows, so a fixed model can cross the interpolations threshold during training. For Resnet18 and CIFAR-10 with 20\% label noise (their Figure~9), this yields three regimes. A small model ($k=3$) never reaches the threshold, and its test error decreases monotonically. An intermediate model ($k=12$) only just reaches it and follows a classical U-curve. A large model ($k=64$) crosses it early, so its test error falls, rises and falls again, eventually below the first minimum: training longer corrects overfitting. Their Figure~10 shows that
the peak grows with label noise and is absent without it.

We summarize each trajectory by its first minimum $E(t_1)$, the
subsequent peak $E(t_p)$ and the final error $E(T)$. The weak form of the large-model claim requires $E(T)<E(t_p)$, i.e. a second descent. The strong form requires $E(T)<E(t_1)$. All three regimes appear within our 400 epochs (Table~\ref{tab:epochwise}, Figure~\ref{fig:epochwise}). The $k=3$ error decreases monotonically to 28.9\%. The $k=12$ error reaches 20.7\% at epoch 80 and then rises to 29.7\% at epoch 400, still increasing. The $k=64$ error falls to 15.1\% at epoch 20, peaks at 26.1\% at epoch 70 and descends to 20.9\% at epoch 400. Finally, the $k=64$ peak falls in the epoch range of the paper's ResNet peaks. Thus, the weak form holds. However, the strong form does not hold as the final error is still 5.8 points above the first minimum. 

The authors train for 4,000 epochs. Their released $k=64$ trajectory is at 21.9\% at epoch 400 and reaches 19.6\% at epoch 4,000. The second descent therefore continues well beyond our horizon, and the missing strong form is not supported as 20\% label noise in both ours and the authors experiments. The papers only evidence of the strong form comes from the 10\% runs (Fig. 10 in the paper).  C3 is therefore partially reproduced. The three regimes and the second descent are reproduced, while \textit{"training longer corrects overfitting"} is shown in its weak from for 20\% label noise, while the strong form for 10\% label noise is untested rather than contradicted. 

The width-128 CNN arm of Figure~10 is shown in Appendix~\ref{app:matched-figure_10} for 0\% and 20\% label noise, which verifies the authors additional claim that the epoch-wise double descent phenomenon holds for different architectures in the presence of label noise. While without label noise, no epoch-wise double descent occurs.

%% Sample-wise non-monotonicity
\subsection{Sample-wise, CNN}\label{sec:samplesize}
Paper Figures 11(a) and 12 motivate the sample-size comparisons.
In~\cite{nakkiran2021deepdouble} they argue that more samples have two
effects on the test error; it falls overall, but the peak moves to larger
widths, since fitting more samples requires a larger model. For CNNs they
only claim that the two effects cancel near the threshold, such that
4$\times$ more samples does not help, whereas more data hurting is shown
for Transformers.

The train error is what ties the peak to the sample size. Taking the
interpolation threshold as the smallest width with train error at most
0.1, the $\epsilon$ of their Definition~2.1, the threshold grows with the
sample size in both the authors' grid and ours, and the test-error peak
lies close to it (\autoref{tab:samplewise}). The moving peak is thus
reproduced (C2).

The plateau (C4) is not, at least not at the same widths. From 12,500 to 50,000
samples the authors' test error falls by 1.9 points at width 32, whereas ours
falls by 10.1, and by 3.3 at width 64, next to our 50,000-sample threshold. With
a fixed budget of 50,000 updates the three sets get 512, 256 and 128 epochs, so
the largest set is the furthest from fitting; its threshold therefore lies
further right than the authors', and the plateau, if present, moves with it. Our
12,500 and 25,000 grids have no widths between 32 and 64, so where the curves
come closest is not measured. \autoref{app:matched-figure_11a}
and~\autoref{app:matched-figure_12} show the curves.

Appendix B.4 states 500K epochs for Figure 11(a), where Section 4 states
500K gradient steps; read as steps, the authors' sets get 5,120 to 1,280
epochs, ten times ours.

We were not able to reproduce their result where more data can actually hurt performance, as the 18k-sample curve remains below the 4k-sample curve across all tested dimensions, as seen in Figure \ref{fig:translation-figure3}. So we partly reproduce the results of model-wise double descent, but we do not reproduce the result of more data hurting performance.

\subsubsection{Sample-wise non-monotonicity}
In Figure 11(b), the authors plot the result of an experiment where they instead vary the number of samples, from 2k to 50k, for two models with dimensions 64 and 80. The results in the paper show a non-monotonic region, where increasing the number of samples actually causes worse performance, and then continuing to increase samples beyond that point gives a monotone decrease in the loss. This result supports the authors claim that near the threshold, more training data does not help and can in fact hurt.

Our attempt to reproduce these results were partly successful, and we see that for our $d_{model}=80$ model, the test loss rises from 4k samples to 6k samples, and we need 12k samples before the loss is lower than at 4k. The $d_{model}=64$ model shows a weaker initial increase in loss from 2k to 4k samples. So we qualitatively partly reproduce the sample-wise non-monotonicity, but our results differ from the paper in the exact positions of the peaks, and the absolute losses also do not match. \subsection{Ambiguities in the paper and released artefacts}
One clear limitation in terms of reproducibility is the lack of a well-maintained codebase. The authors release their model definitions and raw measurements, which made the direct comparisons in this report possible, but the lack of a complete
training pipeline lead to some general ambiguities which affected reproducibility.

\subsubsection{Translation experiments}
The high-level transformer setup was documented, but the exact training procedure was not recoverable. We therefore had to make some guesses and choices of our own. The authors stated that they use a Fairseq style setup, but it is not clear exactly which version or configuration they use. We were also unable to obtain their exact preprocessed files, so the preprocessing may be slightly different, although our data is processed using BPE as they also describe. Is is also unknown exactly which subsets of the data and seeds they used. There was inconsistency in the information about the optimizer between the paper and the authors' codebase. The paper mentions Adam and following the optimization procedure of Vaswani et al. which uses Adam, but the codebase specifies that the translation experiments used SGD. Here we chose to treat the paper as authoritative. It was also confusing exactly which metric they plot, as they write per-token perplexity in the caption of figure 3, but on the actual plot they write Cross-Entropy Test Loss. Since perplexity is the exponential of the cross-entropy, it seems like they did in fact plot cross-entropy, which we did as well. 
The batching strategy turned out to be important for reproducibility. Switching from random greedy batching to a more Fairseq-like length-based batching changed the effective amount of training per update and made the sample-wise non-monotonicity in Figure 11(b) much clearer.
Therefore implementation details affected whether the phenomenon appeared, but we can not necessarily say that they single handedly cause the discrepancy between the results.

\subsection{Effect of the reduced protocol}
Training for at most a tenth of the original horizon lowers the EMC of every procedure we run, so interpolation thresholds appear at larger widths than in the paper. How much this matters depends on the claim. Statements about the existence and ordering of peaks survive. Statements about where curves for different settings meet, or how far a trajectory eventually descends, do not.

In the epoch-wise experiment the reduction truncates the trajectories rather than distorting them. All three regimes, and the $k=64$ peak at epoch 70, develop within the first 400 epochs. At epoch 400 our test errors lie within one point of the authors' for $k=3$ and $k=64$ (Table~\ref{tab:epochwise}). What the truncation removes is the tail of the second descent, which is slow: the authors' $k=64$ run improves by only 2.3 points between epochs 400 and 4,000. The horizon also decides
which regime a width belongs to, because the EMC grows with training time. At 400 epochs our 20\%-noise threshold is
$\hat{k}_{\mathrm{interp}}=16$, so $k=12$ has not yet interpolated, consistent with its intermediate role. The authors' $k=12$ error is flat between epochs 400 and 4,000 (34.1 to 34.6\%), which indicates that the U-curve is a stable regime rather than a truncated double descent.
\\ \\
In addition, reducing the horizon is not neutral across sample sizes. With a fixed number of updates a larger set is seen fewer times, so the reduction moves its threshold the most; the moving peak survives this, whereas the plateau, which depends on where the curves for different sizes meet, does
not stay at the authors' widths. A reduced sample-wise reproduction should therefore give the largest set enough updates to fit, and shorten the smaller sets instead.

\subsection{Limitations}
All results rest on one seed per setting. Seed variance is expected to be largest near the interpolation threshold, where the paper attributes the peak to a single noise-sensitive interpolating model. Our data are consistent with this: at epoch 400 our errors are within one point of the authors' for $k=3$ and $k=64$, but 4.4 points lower for the near-critical $k=12$. Peak heights, and gaps such as the 1 to 3.5 points behind the sample-wise verdict, are therefore not statistically established. We also could not identify the five trials behind the published sample-wise panel among the released files.

Further limitations:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Our width grids resolve peak locations only to the grid spacing, e.g.\ between widths 8, 12 and 16 for ResNet18.
  \item We use 10 and 20\% label noise instead of the 15\% of Figures~1 and~2, and one dataset per domain.
  \item The early-stopping curves (C5) take the minimum over test evaluations, an oracle that overstates what validation-based stopping would achieve.
  \item The exact Fairseq configuration, preprocessing and batching of the translation experiments could not be recovered.
\end{itemize}
Our results should therefore be read as a qualitative rather than an exact numerical reproduction.

\section{Conclusion}

Our reproductions of the translation experiments provide partial support for the paper's results. We reproduced the sample-wise non-monotonicity of Figure 11(b) and the model-wise double descent of Figure 3, but not the region where 4k samples outperform 18k samples. More importantly, our experiments showed that missing details about implementation, in this case especially the batching, can affect whether the reported phenomenon appears and can limit exact reproducibility.
