\begin{abstract}
We replicate a subset of the experiments in ``Deep Double Descent: Where Bigger Models and More Data Hurt'' by Nakkiran et al. Building on the authors' released results and codebase, we reproduce all vision experiments albeit at lower epoch size, and compare them with the authors' released measurements. We further re-implement the IWSLT'14 Transformer experiments. To fit our budget, we train for at most a tenth of the original horizon with one seed per setting (229 vision runs, 14.98 B200 GPU-hours). Model-wise double descent under label noise, and the shift of the interpolation threshold with noise, augmentation and sample size, are reproduced qualitatively. Epoch-wise double descent is replicated for sufficiently large models, while small and medium sized models follow a monotone and U-shaped test error trajectories. The CNN plateau, where more data does not help, does not appear at the authors' widths, which we attribute to the fixed update budget. The noise-free peaks reported for CIFAR-100 are not confirmed at our horizons. [Translation: one sentence missing?ok?]

\section{Introduction}
Classical learning theory predicts a U-shaped bias-variance trade-off, where larger models overfit beyond some complexity. Modern networks contradict this, generalising better as they grow far beyond the number of training samples. Which regime applies matters in practice. It affects whether to enlarge a model, train it longer, stop early or collect more data.
\\ \\
Nakkiran et al.~\cite{nakkiran2021deepdouble} reconcile the two views through the \emph{effective model complexity} (EMC) of a training procedure: the largest number of samples it can fit to near-zero training error (Section~\ref{sec:background}). They hypothesize that test error behaves atypically when the EMC is close to the number of training samples $n$. Varying the width, the training time or $n$ moves a model through this critical regime. The results are \emph{model-wise} and \emph{epoch-wise} double descent, and \emph{sample-wise} non-monotonicity, where more data
can hurt. The authors demonstrate these effects for CNNs, ResNets and Transformers on CIFAR-10/100 and machine translation. We organize our reproduction around five empirical findings described in the paper, labelled C1--C5 below:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[C1] Model-wise double descent exhibits a test-error peak near the interpolation threshold~\cite[Section~2, p.~6; Section~5]{nakkiran2021deepdouble}.
  \item[C2] Adding label noise or data augmentation, or increasing the training-set size, shifts the interpolation threshold and the associated test-error peak towards larger models~\cite[Section~5, p.~8]{nakkiran2021deepdouble}.
  \item[C3] As training proceeds, small, intermediate and large models can exhibit monotonically decreasing, U-shaped and double-descent test-error trajectories, respectively~\cite[Section~6, p.~10]{nakkiran2021deepdouble}.
  \item[C4] Near the critical regime, increasing the training-set size may provide little benefit and, in some settings, worsen test performance~\cite[Section~7, pp.~11--12]{nakkiran2021deepdouble}.
  \item[C5] Optimal early stopping suppresses double descent in many tested settings, but exceptions remain~\cite[Section~8, p.~15]{nakkiran2021deepdouble}.
\end{enumerate}

We treat C1, C3 and C4 as the central empirical results because they
describe the model-wise, epoch-wise and sample-wise behavior that the EMC
framework connects. C2 and C5 are supporting findings about shifts in the
critical region and the effect of early stopping. This distinction guides
our assessment of the reproduction.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we write an independent PyTorch training and evaluation codebase based on the best-effort interpretation of the information in the paper. Second, we replicate the model-, epoch- and sample-wise vision experiments on CIFAR-10/100 and compare them directly with the authors' released measurements. We also re-implement the IWSLT'14 Transformer experiments of their Figures~3 and~11(b). Third, we report the compute cost of this reduced protocol.
Appendix~\ref{app:comparisons} collects paired plots comparing our results
with the authors' results for each reproduced figure, allowing direct
visual comparison across the experimental settings.

Among the central findings, C1 receives qualitative support in noisy vision
and translation width sweeps (Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_04}
and~\ref{fig:translation-figure3}), while the noise-free CIFAR-100 peaks
remain unconfirmed (Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_04}
and~\ref{app:matched-figure_07}). C3 is partially reproduced
(Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_09}); C4 has mixed support, with
sample-wise non-monotonicity in translation but no matching CNN plateau
(Figures~\ref{fig:translation-figure11b} and~\ref{app:matched-figure_11a}).
Among the supporting findings, C2 receives qualitative support
(Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_04}, \ref{app:matched-figure_05}
and~\ref{app:matched-figure_12}), and the opening comparison is consistent
with C5 (Figure~\ref{fig:opening}).


\section{Background}\label{sec:background}
A training procedure $\mathcal{T}$ maps a training set
$S=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ to a classifier $\mathcal{T}(S)$. It covers
architecture, optimizer, learning-rate schedule, number of steps and
augmentation. For a data distribution $\mathcal{D}$ and tolerance
$\epsilon>0$, the EMC is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{EMC}_{\mathcal{D},\epsilon}(\mathcal{T})
  = \max\bigl\{n \mid \mathbb{E}_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^n}
    [\mathrm{Error}_S(\mathcal{T}(S))] \le \epsilon\bigr\},
  \label{eq:emc}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{Error}_S$ is the error on the training set itself and the
authors use $\epsilon=0.1$. Their generalized double-descent hypothesis distinguishes three regimes. If $\mathrm{EMC}\ll n$ (under-parameterized) or $\mathrm{EMC}\gg n$ (over-parameterized), any change that increases the EMC decreases test error. If $\mathrm{EMC}\approx n$ (critically parameterized), such a change may either increase or decrease it. The \emph{interpolation threshold} is the point where $\mathrm{EMC}=n$. Since the EMC cannot be computed directly, we follow the authors and estimate the threshold as the smallest width $\hat{k}_{\mathrm{interp}}$ whose final training error on the (possibly noisy) training labels is at most $\epsilon$.

Increasing width or training time raises the EMC and moves a model through the threshold. Increasing $n$, adding label noise or adding augmentation makes the training set harder to fit and shifts the threshold to larger models. Model-, epoch- and sample-wise double descent (C1, C3, C4) are thus one-dimensional slices through the same picture, and C2 describes how that picture shifts.
The paper investigates these relationships by varying model size, training duration, and training-set size, each of which changes effective model complexity relative to the number of training samples.

paper's inverse-square-root learning rate schedule.

\subsubsection{Experiment selection}
For the translation experiments we choose to try to reproduce Figure 3 and Figure 11b in the paper. They both use the IWSLT'14 German $\rightarrow$ English dataset and the same transformer family. They contain complimentary experiments: Figure 3 varies $d_{model}$ while comparing 4k and 18k samples, and Figure 11b holds $d_{model}$ fixed at 64 and 80 and varies the sample size. \subsubsection{Experimental setup and reproduction choices}
The description of the experimental setup is limited, but the authors describe that they use the IWSLT'14 German $\rightarrow$ English dataset. They train a 6-layer encoder-decoder Transformer with 8 attention heads. The model size varies in the experiments with $d_{model}$, and the feed-forward dimension is $d_{ff}=4d_{model}$. They use 10\% label smoothing and no dropout, and they train for 80k gradient updates. The IWSLT text was preprocessed using Byte-Pair-Encodings, and they used the Fairseq sequence-modeling toolkit for the Transformer implementation. Training follows the optimization procedure of Vaswani et al.~\cite{vaswani2017attention}, with a linear warmup phase followed by inverse-square-root learning-rate decay using Adam. \\

Because the experiments were not fully specified in the report nor in their code repository, so we had to make some choices. We used the optimization procedure of Vaswani et al. \cite{nakkiran2021deepdouble}, using Adam with $(\beta_1,\beta_2)=(0.9,0.98)$ and $\epsilon=10^{-9}$. We used 4000 warmup steps, and learning rate $lr=d_{model}^{-0.5}\cdot\min(step\_num^{-0.5},step\_num\cdot warmup\_steps^{-1.5})$. So the learning rate increases gradually during the warmup, and then decays proportional to the square root of the step number. \\
For batches we used a max token budget of $4096$, rather than a fixed number of sentences. Batches are then formed from similarly sized sentences using a Fairseq-like length-based batching procedure. Therefore the number of sentences per batch vary with sentence length. \\

For figure 3 we used 25 widths, evenly spaced from 8 to 200, so $d_{model}=\{8,16,...,192,200\}$, with 4k and 18k training samples. For figure 11b we used $d_{model}=\{64,80\}$ and $n=2k,4k...,48k,50k$. So our setup is Fairseq-like, but we do not know for sure if it is exactly the same as the authors.

\subsection{Model-wise Double Descent}\label{sec:modelwise}

\subsubsection{Model size, label noise, and optimization}
The model-wise experiments test how test error rises and then falls as
model width increases near the interpolation threshold (C1; Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_04}), and whether
label noise and augmentation shift this region towards larger models
(C2; Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_04} and~\ref{app:matched-figure_05}).
Our ResNet18 sweeps reproduce a local test-error peak followed by a second
descent under 10\% and 20\% label noise on both CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100
(Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_04}). The corresponding noise-free curves
decrease across the tested widths (Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_04}). Increasing label noise also shifts the
training-error threshold towards larger widths, with the test-error peak
generally moving in the same direction (Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_04}). These results qualitatively support
C1 and the label-noise component of C2, although our sparse width grid limits
how precisely the peak can be located relative to the threshold.

The CNN results show that this pattern depends on the training setting.
Without augmentation, the CIFAR-10 curves on noisy data show a pronounced peak
followed by decreasing test error (Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_05}). With
augmentation, fitting the training labels requires larger widths, and the
test-error's rise occurs further to the right (Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_05}). However, the augmented
20\%-noise curve shows only a slight decline after its peak within the
displayed range: test error rises from 21.74\% at width 16 to 28.11\% at
width 48, then falls slightly by width 64 after 50,000 updates
(Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_05}). This supports a shift in the critical
region under augmentation (C2), while providing weaker evidence for a
completed second descent (C1; Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_05} and~\ref{app:matched-figure_12}). The noise-free CIFAR-100 CNN peak reported
by the authors is not resolved in our reproduction
(Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_07}). For clean CIFAR-10, test error decreases
with width under both SGD and Adam (Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_06}).

The minimum observed test-error envelope in the opening comparison
(Figure~\ref{fig:opening}) largely suppresses the width-dependent peak,
consistent with C5. This is an oracle comparison using test evaluations,
however, and does not establish that validation-based early stopping would
achieve the same result. An epoch- or step-matched comparison of the results visualized in Figures 1-12 of the original paper, alongside the auhtors results, can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:comparisons}.

%% Epoch-wise double descent (C3)
\subsection{Epoch-wise Double Descent}
\label{sec:epochwise}
Nakkiran et al. argue that traning time acts like model size. As traning proceeds the EMC grows, so a fixed model can cross the interpolations threshold during training. For Resnet18 and CIFAR-10 with 20\% label noise (their Figure~9), this yields three regimes. A small model ($k=3$) never reaches the threshold, and its test error decreases monotonically. An intermediate model ($k=12$) only just reaches it and follows a classical U-curve. A large model ($k=64$) crosses it early, so its test error falls, rises and falls again, eventually below the first minimum: training longer corrects overfitting. Their Figure~10 shows that
the peak grows with label noise and is absent without it.

We summarize each trajectory by its first minimum $E(t_1)$, the
subsequent peak $E(t_p)$ and the final error $E(T)$. The weak form of the large-model claim requires $E(T)<E(t_p)$, i.e. a second descent. The strong form requires $E(T)<E(t_1)$. All three regimes appear within our 400 epochs (Table~\ref{tab:epochwise}, Figure~\ref{fig:epochwise}). The $k=3$ error decreases monotonically to 28.9\%. The $k=12$ error reaches 20.7\% at epoch 80 and then rises to 29.7\% at epoch 400, still increasing. The $k=64$ error falls to 15.1\% at epoch 20, peaks at 26.1\% at epoch 70 and descends to 20.9\% at epoch 400. Finally, the $k=64$ peak falls in the epoch range of the paper's ResNet peaks (Figure~\ref{fig:epochwise}). Thus, the weak form holds. However, the strong form does not hold as the final error is still 5.8 points above the first minimum. 

The authors train for 4,000 epochs. Their released $k=64$ trajectory is at 21.9\% at epoch 400 and reaches 19.6\% at epoch 4,000 (Figure~\ref{fig:epochwise}; Table~\ref{tab:epochwise}). The second descent therefore continues well beyond our horizon, and the missing strong form is not supported as 20\% label noise in both ours and the authors experiments. The papers only evidence of the strong form comes from the 10\% runs (Fig. 10 in the paper).  C3 is therefore partially reproduced. The three regimes and the second descent are reproduced, while \textit{"training longer corrects overfitting"} is shown in its weak from for 20\% label noise, while the strong form for 10\% label noise is untested rather than contradicted. 

The width-128 CNN arm of Figure~10 is shown in Appendix~\ref{app:matched-figure_10} for 0\% and 20\% label noise, which verifies the authors additional claim that the epoch-wise double descent phenomenon holds for different architectures in the presence of label noise. The clean CNN trajectory does not show epoch-wise double descent (Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_10}).

%% Sample-wise non-monotonicity
\subsection{Sample-wise, CNN}\label{sec:samplesize}
Paper Figures 11(a) and 12 motivate the sample-size comparisons.
In~\cite{nakkiran2021deepdouble} they argue that more samples have two
effects on the test error; it falls overall, but the peak moves to larger
widths, since fitting more samples requires a larger model. For CNNs they
only claim that the two effects cancel near the threshold, such that
4$\times$ more samples does not help, whereas more data hurting is shown
for Transformers.

The train error is what ties the peak to the sample size. Taking the
interpolation threshold as the smallest width with train error at most
0.1, the $\epsilon$ of their Definition~2.1, the threshold grows with the
sample size in both the authors' grid and ours, and the test-error peak
lies close to it (\autoref{tab:samplewise}). The moving peak is thus
reproduced (C2; Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_11a} and~\ref{app:matched-figure_12}).

The plateau (C4) is not, at least not at the same widths. From 12,500 to 50,000
samples the authors' test error falls by 1.9 points at width 32, whereas ours
falls by 10.1, and by 3.3 at width 64, next to our 50,000-sample threshold (Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_11a}; Table~\ref{tab:samplewise}). With
a fixed budget of 50,000 updates the three sets get 512, 256 and 128 epochs, so
the largest set is the furthest from fitting; its threshold therefore lies
further right than the authors', and the plateau, if present, moves with it. Our
12,500 and 25,000 grids have no widths between 32 and 64, so where the curves
come closest is not measured. \autoref{app:matched-figure_11a}
and~\autoref{app:matched-figure_12} show the curves.

Appendix B.4 states 500K epochs for Figure 11(a), where Section 4 states
500K gradient steps; read as steps, the authors' sets get 5,120 to 1,280
epochs, ten times ours.

\subsection{Translation results}
\label{sec:translation-res}

\subsubsection{Model-wise double descent}
In the original paper, figure 3 showed that for both 4k and 18k samples, test-loss initially increased with $d_{model}$, but after peaking around $d_{model}=24$ for 4k samples, and $d_{model}=56$ for 18k samples, the test loss decreased with increased dimension continuing decreasing even at $d_{model}=200$, which fits with the authors broader claim that increasing sample size can shift the critical region towards larger models. The peak in test loss is also observed in our results, but earlier for both 4k and 18k samples. We were not able to reproduce their result where more data can actually hurt performance, as the 18k-sample curve remains below the 4k-sample curve across all tested dimensions, as seen in Figure \ref{fig:translation-figure3}. So we partly reproduce the results of model-wise double descent, but we do not reproduce the result of more data hurting performance (Figure~\ref{fig:translation-figure3}).



\subsubsection{Sample-wise non-monotonicity}
In Figure 11(b), the authors plot the result of an experiment where they instead vary the number of samples, from 2k to 50k, for two models with dimensions 64 and 80. The results in the paper show a non-monotonic region, where increasing the number of samples actually causes worse performance, and then continuing to increase samples beyond that point gives a monotone decrease in the loss. This result supports the authors claim that near the threshold, more training data does not help and can in fact hurt.

Our attempt to reproduce these results were partly successful, and we see that for our $d_{model}=80$ model, the test loss rises from 4k samples to 6k samples, and we need 12k samples before the loss is lower than at 4k (Figure~\ref{fig:translation-figure11b}). The $d_{model}=64$ model shows a weaker initial increase in loss from 2k to 4k samples (Figure~\ref{fig:translation-figure11b}). So we qualitatively partly reproduce the sample-wise non-monotonicity, but our results differ from the paper in the exact positions of the peaks, and the absolute losses also do not match. The results are shown in Figure \ref{fig:translation-figure11b}.

\subsection{Ambiguities in the paper and released artefacts}
One clear limitation in terms of reproducibility is the lack of a well-maintained codebase. The authors release their model definitions and raw measurements, which made the direct comparisons in this report possible, but the lack of a complete
training pipeline lead to some general ambiguities which affected reproducibility.

\subsubsection{Translation experiments}
The high-level transformer setup was documented, but the exact training procedure was not recoverable. We therefore had to make some guesses and choices of our own. The authors stated that they use a Fairseq style setup, but it is not clear exactly which version or configuration they use. We were also unable to obtain their exact preprocessed files, so the preprocessing may be slightly different, although our data is processed using BPE as they also describe. Is is also unknown exactly which subsets of the data and seeds they used. There was inconsistency in the information about the optimizer between the paper and the authors' codebase. The paper mentions Adam and following the optimization procedure of Vaswani et al. which uses Adam, but the codebase specifies that the translation experiments used SGD. Here we chose to treat the paper as authoritative. It was also confusing exactly which metric they plot, as they write per-token perplexity in the caption of figure 3, but on the actual plot they write Cross-Entropy Test Loss. Since perplexity is the exponential of the cross-entropy, it seems like they did in fact plot cross-entropy, which we did as well. 
The batching strategy turned out to be important for reproducibility. Switching from random greedy batching to a more Fairseq-like length-based batching changed the effective amount of training per update and made the sample-wise non-monotonicity in Figure 11(b) much clearer.
Therefore implementation details affected whether the phenomenon appeared, but we can not necessarily say that they single handedly cause the discrepancy between the results.

\subsection{Effect of the reduced protocol}
The model-wise results indicate that our reduced protocol preserves some
qualitative patterns but does not reproduce every setting equally well.
The noisy ResNet18 sweeps show peaks and subsequent descents resembling
the authors' measurements at the displayed 400-epoch horizon
(Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_04}). The augmented CNN sweeps provide less
complete evidence: training error remains appreciable across much of the
width range, and the second descent is only partially observed
(Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_05} and~\ref{app:matched-figure_12}). Shorter
training is a plausible contributor, but our experiments do not isolate its
effect from preprocessing, initialization and other implementation
differences. The missing noise-free CIFAR-100 peak therefore remains
inconclusive (Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_07}).

In the epoch-wise experiment the reduction truncates the trajectories rather than distorting them. All three regimes, and the $k=64$ peak at epoch 70, develop within the first 400 epochs (Figure~\ref{fig:epochwise}). At epoch 400 our test errors lie within one point of the authors' for $k=3$ and $k=64$ (Table~\ref{tab:epochwise}). What the truncation removes is the tail of the second descent, which is slow: the authors' $k=64$ run improves by only 2.3 points between epochs 400 and 4,000 (Figure~\ref{fig:epochwise}). The horizon also decides
which regime a width belongs to, because the EMC grows with training time. At 400 epochs our 20\%-noise threshold is
$\hat{k}_{\mathrm{interp}}=16$ (Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_04}), so $k=12$ has not yet interpolated, consistent with its intermediate role. The authors' $k=12$ error is flat between epochs 400 and 4,000 (34.1 to 34.6\%), which indicates that the U-curve is a stable regime rather than a truncated double descent (Figure~\ref{fig:epochwise}).
\\ \\
In addition, reducing the horizon is not neutral across sample sizes. With a fixed number of updates a larger set is seen fewer times, so the reduction moves its threshold the most; the moving peak survives this, whereas the plateau, which depends on where the curves for different sizes meet, does
not stay at the authors' widths (Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_11a} and~\ref{app:matched-figure_12}). A reduced sample-wise reproduction should therefore give the largest set enough updates to fit, and shorten the smaller sets instead.

\subsection{Limitations}
All results rest on one seed per setting. Seed variance is expected to be largest near the interpolation threshold, where the paper attributes the peak to a single noise-sensitive interpolating model. Our data are consistent with this: at epoch 400 our errors are within one point of the authors' for $k=3$ and $k=64$, but 4.4 points lower for the near-critical $k=12$ (Figure~\ref{fig:epochwise}; Table~\ref{tab:epochwise}). The observed peak heights and the 1--3.5 percentage-point differences in the sample-wise experiments come from single runs, so we cannot determine whether they persist across random seeds. Although the published sample-wise panel averages five trials, we could not identify those trials in the released files.

Further limitations:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Our width grids resolve peak locations only to the grid spacing, e.g.\ between widths 8, 12 and 16 for ResNet18.
  \item We use 10 and 20\% label noise instead of the 15\% of Figures~1 and~2, and one dataset per domain.
  \item The early-stopping curves (C5; Figure~\ref{fig:opening}) take the minimum over test evaluations, an oracle that overstates what validation-based stopping would achieve.
  \item The exact Fairseq configuration, preprocessing and batching of the translation experiments could not be recovered.
\end{itemize}
Our results should therefore be read as a qualitative rather than an exact numerical reproduction.

In addition, some implementation details from the original setup, including the exact Fairseq version, preprocessing, and batching configuration, could not be recovered exactly. Our results should therefore be interpreted as a qualitative rather than exact numerical reproduction.

\section{Conclusion}

For the central empirical findings, our reproduction provides qualitative
but incomplete support. Model-wise double descent (C1) appears in the
noisy vision sweeps and the translation width sweeps
(Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_04} and~\ref{fig:translation-figure3}),
while the noise-free CIFAR-100 peaks remain unconfirmed
(Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_04} and~\ref{app:matched-figure_07}). For epoch-wise double descent (C3),
we reproduce the three training-time regimes and a second descent at
20\% label noise, but do not establish improvement below the first minimum
(Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_09}).
For sample-wise non-monotonicity (C4), the translation sample-size sweep
shows regions where more data hurts (Figure~\ref{fig:translation-figure11b}),
whereas the CNN plateau is not reproduced at the authors' widths
(Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_11a} and~\ref{app:matched-figure_12}).
The translation width sweep also fails to reproduce the region where
4,000 samples outperform 18,000 (Figure~\ref{fig:translation-figure3}).

The supporting findings receive qualified support. The vision experiments
show shifts of the critical region with label noise, augmentation and
sample size (C2; Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_04},
\ref{app:matched-figure_05} and~\ref{app:matched-figure_12}).
The minimum observed test-error envelope suppresses the peak in our
opening comparison (Figure~\ref{fig:opening}), consistent with C5,
although it uses test
evaluations rather than validation-based stopping. Together, these results
support the EMC framework's qualitative account across model size,
training duration and sample size. Reduced vision training horizons,
sparse grids, single-seed experiments and unresolved implementation details
limit exact reproduction. In particular, the translation experiments show
that batching choices can affect whether sample-wise non-monotonicity
appears.
