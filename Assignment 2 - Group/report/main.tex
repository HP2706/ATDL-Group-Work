\begin{abstract}
We reproduce selected experiments from ``Deep Double Descent: Where Bigger Models and More Data Hurt'' by Nakkiran et al. We reuse the released vision model definitions, implement training and evaluation, and compare our CIFAR-10/100 results with the authors' released measurements. Vision experiments use one seed per setting and reduced training horizons: 400 Adam epochs or 50,000 SGD updates, corresponding to one tenth of the stated horizons, or one twentieth for the CIFAR-100 CNN. Noisy width sweeps qualitatively reproduce model-wise double descent and shifts of the interpolation threshold with label noise, augmentation and sample size. Small, intermediate and large ResNet18 models exhibit decreasing, U-shaped and double-descent test-error trajectories, respectively, although the large model's final error remains above its first minimum. The CNN plateau is not reproduced at the authors' widths; unequal training progress under the reduced update budget is a plausible explanation. Clean CIFAR-100 peaks remain unconfirmed. At the original training horizon, IWSLT'14 experiments show non-monotonic test loss with width and sample size, but do not reproduce the width-sweep region where 18,000 examples perform worse than 4,000. These results provide meaningful qualitative but incomplete support for the selected findings.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Classical learning theory predicts a U-shaped bias-variance trade-off, where larger models overfit beyond some complexity. Modern networks contradict this, generalising better as they grow far beyond the number of training samples. Which regime applies matters in practice. It affects whether to enlarge a model, train it longer, stop early or collect more data.
\\ \\
Nakkiran et al.~\cite{nakkiran2021deepdouble} reconcile the two views through the \emph{effective model complexity} (EMC) of a training procedure: the largest number of samples it can fit to near-zero training error (Section~\ref{sec:background}). They hypothesize that test error behaves atypically when the EMC is close to the number of training samples $n$. Varying the width, the training time or $n$ moves a model through this critical regime. The results are \emph{model-wise} and \emph{epoch-wise} double descent, and \emph{sample-wise} non-monotonicity, where more data
can hurt. The authors demonstrate these effects for CNNs, ResNets and Transformers on CIFAR-10/100 and machine translation. We organize our reproduction around what we believe are the five main empirical findings described in the paper, labelled C1--C5 below:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[C1] Model-wise double descent exhibits a test-error peak near the interpolation threshold~\cite[Section~2, p.~6; Section~5]{nakkiran2021deepdouble}.
  \item[C2] Adding label noise or data augmentation, or increasing the training-set size, shifts the interpolation threshold and the associated test-error peak towards larger models~\cite[Section~5, p.~8]{nakkiran2021deepdouble}.
  \item[C3] As training proceeds, small, intermediate and large models can exhibit monotonically decreasing, U-shaped and double-descent test-error trajectories, respectively~\cite[Section~6, p.~10]{nakkiran2021deepdouble}.
  \item[C4] Near the critical regime, increasing the training-set size may provide little benefit and, in some settings, worsen test performance~\cite[Section~7, pp.~11--12]{nakkiran2021deepdouble}.
  \item[C5] Optimal early stopping suppresses double descent in many tested settings, but exceptions remain~\cite[Section~8, p.~15]{nakkiran2021deepdouble}.
\end{enumerate}

We treat C1, C3 and C4 as the central empirical results because they
describe the model-wise, epoch-wise and sample-wise behavior that the EMC
framework connects. C2 and C5 are supporting findings about shifts in the
critical region and the effect of early stopping. This distinction guides
our assessment of the reproduction.

Our contributions are twofold. First, we write an independent PyTorch training and evaluation codebase based on the best-effort interpretation of the paper. Second, we replicate the model-, epoch- and sample-wise vision experiments on CIFAR-10/100 and compare them directly with the authors' released measurements. We also re-implement the IWSLT'14 Transformer experiments of their Figures~3 and~11(b).
Appendix~\ref{app:comparisons} collects paired plots comparing our results
with the authors' results for each figure in the main paper, allowing direct
visual comparison across the experimental settings.

Among the central findings, C1 receives qualitative support in noisy vision
and translation width sweeps (Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_04}
and~\ref{fig:translation-figure3}), while the noise-free CIFAR-100 peaks
remain unconfirmed (Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_04}
and~\ref{app:matched-figure_07}). C3 is partially reproduced
(Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_09}); C4 has mixed support, with
sample-wise non-monotonicity in translation but no matching CNN plateau
(Figures~\ref{fig:translation-figure11b} and~\ref{app:matched-figure_11a}).
Among the supporting findings, C2 receives qualitative support
(Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_04}, \ref{app:matched-figure_05}
and~\ref{app:matched-figure_12}), and the opening comparison is consistent
with C5 (Figure~\ref{fig:opening}).


\section{Background}\label{sec:background}
A training procedure $\mathcal{T}$ maps a training set
$S=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ to a classifier $\mathcal{T}(S)$. It covers
architecture, optimizer, learning-rate schedule, number of steps and
augmentation. For a data distribution $\mathcal{D}$ and tolerance
$\epsilon>0$, the EMC is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{EMC}_{\mathcal{D},\epsilon}(\mathcal{T})
  = \max\bigl\{n \mid \mathbb{E}_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^n}
    [\mathrm{Error}_S(\mathcal{T}(S))] \le \epsilon\bigr\},
  \label{eq:emc}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{Error}_S$ is the error on the training set itself and the
authors use $\epsilon=0.1$. Their generalized double-descent hypothesis distinguishes three regimes. If $\mathrm{EMC}\ll n$ (under-parameterized) or $\mathrm{EMC}\gg n$ (over-parameterized), any change that increases the EMC decreases test error. If $\mathrm{EMC}\approx n$ (critically parameterized), such a change may either increase or decrease it. The \emph{interpolation threshold} is the point where $\mathrm{EMC}=n$. Since the EMC cannot be computed directly, we follow the authors and estimate the threshold as the smallest width $\hat{k}_{\mathrm{interp}}$ whose final training error on the (possibly noisy) training labels is at most $\epsilon$.

Increasing width or training time raises the EMC and moves a model through the threshold. Increasing $n$, adding label noise or adding augmentation makes the training set harder to fit and shifts the threshold to larger models. Model-, epoch- and sample-wise double descent (C1, C3, C4) are thus one-dimensional slices through the same picture, and C2 describes how that picture shifts.
The paper investigates these relationships by varying model size, training duration, and training-set size, each of which changes effective model complexity relative to the number of training samples.

\section{Methodology and experimental design}
\subsection{Vision setup}\label{sec:vision-setup}
We reuse the released width-scaled ResNet18 and five-layer CNN definitions
and implement training and evaluation in PyTorch. CIFAR-10/100 sweeps use
one seed, sparse width grids and selected label-noise rates of 0, 10 and
20\%. Adam runs last 400 epochs and SGD runs 50,000 updates, one tenth of
the stated horizons except for the CIFAR-100 CNN, which uses one twentieth.
We evaluate clean test error and training error against fixed, possibly
corrupted labels. Preprocessing, optimizer settings and differences from
the original protocol, including width grids and sample sizes, are specified
in Table~\ref{tab:protocol}. Appendix~\ref{app:coverage} maps the experiments
to the reproduced figures.

Within each width sweep, the noise sensitivity analysis varies label corruption
while holding the dataset, architecture, optimizer, augmentation and training
budget fixed. The augmentation ablation removes random cropping and flipping
at fixed noise rate, widths and SGD update budget. Both comparisons test
whether the training-error threshold and test-error peak shift, and whether
a second descent remains visible.

\subsection{Translation setup}\label{sec:translation-setup}
We reproduce IWSLT'14 German--English experiments using a six-layer
encoder--decoder Transformer. The width sweep uses 25 embedding dimensions
from 8 to 200 at 4,000 and 18,000 training pairs; the sample-size sweep uses
widths 64 and 80 at 2,000 to 50,000 pairs. Both retain the stated
80,000-update horizon. We use Adam with warmup and inverse-square-root
decay, BPE preprocessing and length-based batches of up to 4,096 tokens.
Table~\ref{tab:translation-protocol} records the full configuration and
unresolved details of the authors' setup. We compare the resulting test-loss
curves in Figures~\ref{fig:translation-figure3}
and~\ref{fig:translation-figure11b}.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Model-wise double descent}\label{sec:modelwise}
Our ResNet18 sweeps reproduce a local test-error peak followed by a second
descent under 10\% and 20\% label noise on both CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100
(Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_04}). The noise-free curves decrease with width; increasing label noise shifts
the training-error threshold and generally the test-error peak towards
larger widths (Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_04}). These results qualitatively support
C1 and the label-noise component of C2, although our sparse width grid limits
how precisely the peak can be located relative to the threshold.

Without augmentation, the CIFAR-10 curves on noisy data show a pronounced peak
followed by decreasing test error (Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_05}). With
augmentation, fitting the training labels requires larger widths, and the
test-error's rise occurs further to the right (Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_05}). However, the augmented
20\%-noise curve shows only a slight decline after its peak within the
displayed range: test error rises from 21.74\% at width 16 to 28.11\% at
width 48, then falls slightly by width 64 after 50,000 updates
(Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_05}). This supports a shift in the critical
region under augmentation (C2), while providing weaker evidence for a
completed second descent (C1; Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_05}). The noise-free CIFAR-100 CNN peak reported
by the authors is not resolved in our reproduction
(Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_07}). For clean CIFAR-10, test error decreases
with width under both SGD and Adam (Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_06}).
This qualitative agreement spans both training procedures, but their different
budgets, 50,000 SGD updates versus 400 Adam epochs, prevent isolating the
optimizer's effect.

The minimum observed test-error envelope suppresses the opening comparison's
peak, consistent with C5 (Figure~\ref{fig:opening}). This is an oracle
assessment using test evaluations, not validation-based early stopping.

\subsection{Epoch-wise double descent}\label{sec:epochwise}
We summarize each trajectory by its first minimum $E(t_1)$, subsequent
peak $E(t_p)$ and final error $E(T)$. The weak form of the large-model
claim requires $E(T)<E(t_p)$, i.e.\ a second descent; the strong form
requires $E(T)<E(t_1)$. All three C3 regimes appear within our 400 epochs
(Table~\ref{tab:epochwise}; Figure~\ref{fig:epochwise}). The $k=3$ error
decreases monotonically to 28.9\%. The $k=12$ error reaches 20.7\% at
epoch 80 and then rises to 29.7\% at epoch 400, still increasing. The
$k=64$ error falls to 15.1\% at epoch 20, peaks at 26.1\% at epoch 70 and
descends to 20.9\% at epoch 400. Thus the weak form holds, but the final
error remains 5.8 percentage points above the first minimum
(Figure~\ref{fig:epochwise}; Table~\ref{tab:epochwise}).

The authors train for 4,000 epochs. Their released $k=64$ trajectory is at
21.9\% at epoch 400 and reaches 19.6\% at epoch 4,000, so the second
descent continues well beyond our horizon (Figure~\ref{fig:epochwise};
Table~\ref{tab:epochwise}).
The stronger result of finishing below the first minimum is demonstrated
at 10\% noise in the paper's Figure~10~\cite{nakkiran2021deepdouble}; that
condition is untested in our three-width ResNet comparison. Our width-128
CNN trajectories likewise show a second descent with 20\% noise but not
with clean labels (Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_10}).

\subsection{Sample-wise non-monotonicity, CNN}\label{sec:samplesize}
Increasing the sample size shifts both the training-error threshold and
the test-error peak towards larger CNN widths (C2;
Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_11a} and~\ref{app:matched-figure_12}).
Table~\ref{tab:samplewise} estimates the 10\% training-error crossing by
linear interpolation between widths. Our thresholds increase from 17 to
27 to 56, compared with 15, 23 and 34 in the authors' grid; the peaks move
in the same direction (Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_11a};
Table~\ref{tab:samplewise}).

The sample-wise experiment asks a different question from the moving
peak: at a fixed width, does increasing the dataset improve test error?
The authors' CNN curves contain a region where additional data provides
little benefit (Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_11a}).
The CNN plateau (C4) is not reproduced at the authors' widths. Increasing
sample size from 12,500 to 50,000 reduces their test error by 1.9 percentage
points at width 32, compared with 10.1 points in our runs. At width 64,
our reduction is 3.3 points (Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_11a};
Table~\ref{tab:samplewise}). The 12,500- and 25,000-sample grids have no
widths between 32 and 64, limiting localization of any shifted plateau
(Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_11a} and~\ref{app:matched-figure_12}).

\subsection{Translation results}\label{sec:translation-res}
The translation width sweeps exhibit peaks followed by decreasing loss,
qualitatively supporting the width-dependent pattern in C1, but their peaks
occur at smaller dimensions than the authors'. The 18,000-example curve
remains below the 4,000-example curve throughout, so this experiment does
not reproduce the reported region where more data hurts
(Figure~\ref{fig:translation-figure3}).

The sample-size sweep provides partial support for C4. At width 80, loss
rises between 4,000 and 6,000 examples and falls below its 4,000-example
value only at 12,000; width 64 shows a weaker increase from 2,000 to 4,000.
Peak locations and absolute losses differ from the authors'
(Figure~\ref{fig:translation-figure11b}).

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\subsection{Effect of the reduced protocol}
The reduced training horizon preserves some qualitative patterns. Noisy ResNet18 width sweeps resemble the authors' displayed
400-epoch results, while augmented CNNs retain appreciable training error
and only a partial second descent across the displayed widths
(Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_04} and~\ref{app:matched-figure_05}).
The missing clean CIFAR-100 peaks remain inconclusive because shorter
training, sparse grids and implementation differences are not isolated
(Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_04} and~\ref{app:matched-figure_07}).

At epoch 400 our test errors lie within one percentage point of the authors'
for $k=3$ and $k=64$ (Table~\ref{tab:epochwise}). The authors' $k=64$ run
improves by only 2.3 points over the next 3,600 epochs
(Figure~\ref{fig:epochwise}). The reduced horizon thus removes a slow tail
of the second descent. Observing that descent supports C3 without
establishing that longer training beats an early minimum.

The horizon also affects how a width is classified, because EMC grows with
training time. At 400 epochs our 20\%-noise threshold is
$\hat{k}_{\mathrm{interp}}=16$, so $k=12$ has not yet crossed the threshold
(Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_04}). The authors' $k=12$ error changes
little between epochs 400 and 4,000 (34.1 to 34.6\%), providing no evidence
of a delayed second descent within their longer horizon
(Figure~\ref{fig:epochwise}; Table~\ref{tab:epochwise}).

The fixed update budget also gives larger datasets fewer passes: our
12,500-, 25,000- and 50,000-example CNN runs receive 512, 256 and 128 epochs.
The larger set's threshold is displaced most relative to the authors'
(Table~\ref{tab:samplewise}), so unequal progress towards fitting is a
plausible explanation for the missing plateau (C4;
Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_11a} and~\ref{app:matched-figure_12}).
The moving peak survives the reduction, but the plateau depends on where
the sample-size curves meet (Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_11a}
and~\ref{app:matched-figure_12}). A targeted follow-up would extend
training for the largest set, holding other settings fixed, and add widths
around its shifted threshold.

\subsection{Implementation and statistical uncertainty}
The released vision model definitions and measurements enable comparisons,
but the missing training pipeline leaves preprocessing and evaluation
details unresolved (Table~\ref{tab:protocol}). For translation, the exact
preprocessed data, subsets, seeds and batching configuration could not be
recovered. The paper describes Adam, while the release describes SGD;
we follow the paper. Perplexity and cross-entropy labels are also
inconsistent, limiting interpretation of absolute loss differences
(Table~\ref{tab:translation-protocol}). Our final length-based batching
configuration exhibits sample-wise non-monotonicity, but the displayed
comparison does not isolate batching's effect from other choices
(Figure~\ref{fig:translation-figure11b}).

Single-seed experiments cannot establish the stability of peak heights or
small performance differences, and sparse grids limit threshold
localization (Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_04}
and~\ref{app:matched-figure_11a}). We could not identify the five trials
underlying the published CNN sample-wise panel; our comparison instead
uses the released single-trial grid
(Figure~\ref{app:matched-figure_11a}). Finally, the test-error envelope
provides qualified support for C5 without demonstrating validation-based
stopping (Figure~\ref{fig:opening}). These limitations favor qualitative
rather than exact numerical conclusions.

\section{Conclusion}
The central findings receive qualitative but incomplete support: noisy
width sweeps exhibit double descent (C1; Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_04}
and~\ref{fig:translation-figure3}), the three epoch-wise regimes appear
(C3; Figure~\ref{fig:epochwise}), and translation shows sample-wise
non-monotonicity (C4; Figure~\ref{fig:translation-figure11b}). The CNN
plateau and clean CIFAR-100 peaks remain unresolved
(Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_11a} and~\ref{app:matched-figure_07}).
Supporting findings include shifts of the critical region (C2;
Figures~\ref{app:matched-figure_04}, \ref{app:matched-figure_05}
and~\ref{app:matched-figure_12}) and suppression of the opening peak by
oracle early stopping (C5; Figure~\ref{fig:opening}). The results support
the EMC framework's qualitative predictions, subject to reduced horizons,
single seeds and implementation uncertainty.
